GNSS

GEODETIC PRECISION · Global Navigation Satellite Systems

Tracking the absolute position of a single point on the ground, every day, to the millimetre.

What it measures How it works A real record Strengths & limits How I use it

Where InSAR sees millions of points but only relative motion, GNSS does the opposite: one point, but its position pinned in an absolute, global reference frame — the anchor everything else is measured against.

A continuous GNSS station logs the signals from navigation satellites (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou) around the clock. After careful processing, its daily position is known to a few millimetres, and years of those daily positions reveal exactly how the ground beneath it is moving.

What it measures

Absolute position, in three dimensions

Each daily solution gives the station's East, North, and Up coordinates in a global frame (NGL publishes these in IGS20, a GPS realisation of the international reference frame). Because the frame is tied to the centre of the Earth, the motion is absolute — not relative to a nearby point.

Stack the daily positions into a time series and the slope of each component is the station's velocity. NGL estimates a robust trend with the MIDAS algorithm, which is resistant to the steps and outliers that real records always contain.

How it works

From satellites to a velocity

The receiver measures the travel time of the carrier signal from each visible satellite. Combining many satellites and many days, and modelling the atmosphere, clocks, and orbits, yields a precise daily position. The scatter of those daily points is the day-to-day precision; the trend through them is the signal.

Three things shape every real record: a long-term trend (tectonics, subsidence, rebound), repeating seasonal oscillations (water, temperature, atmospheric loading), and discrete steps from earthquakes or equipment changes that must be modelled out before a clean velocity falls out.

Strengths & limits

Where it shines, where it struggles

Strengths
  • Absolute — position in a global frame, the datum InSAR lacks.
  • Truly 3-D — East, North, and Up resolved independently.
  • Continuous in time — daily samples, often spanning decades.
  • High precision — millimetre-level after processing; robust velocities from long records.
Limits
  • Spatially sparse — one point per station; no continuous map.
  • Costly — each site is a permanent monument and receiver to install and maintain.
  • Steps & gaps — earthquakes, equipment swaps, and outages interrupt the record.
  • Local monument effects — settling or instability of the pillar can mimic ground motion.
How I use it

The datum for InSAR

InSAR gives me dense spatial coverage but only relative motion. GNSS supplies the missing piece: an absolute reference to tie the radar to, and an independent check on the velocities it produces. At InSAR-monitored deformation sites, comparing the line-of-sight rate against the co-located GNSS rate is how I decide whether a track is trustworthy.

The PVEP station sits at the intersection of all three methods within one of my current projects. The Palos Verdes Peninsula in Los Angeles County hosts one of the largest active urban landslide complexes in the United States, where rainfall-driven ground movement has been accelerating and affecting hundreds of homes in Rancho Palos Verdes and surrounding communities. This hobby project combines JPL's ARIA displacement maps and a Sentinel-1 time-series line-of-sight (LOS) velocity layer, CORS GNSS data from stations like PVEP alongside local GPS measurements, a LiDAR-derived DEM for topographic slope and aspect, and weather records. These datasets are fed into a machine-learning model (currently experimenting with a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) approach) to predict which buildings and parcels are most exposed to progressive landslide damage over the next 5 to 10 years.
