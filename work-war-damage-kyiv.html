Selected work · 04

War-related building damage, Kyiv Oblast

Mapping war damage to buildings around Kyiv with medium-resolution Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2 optical imagery — open data, no field access required.

● 58% match vs. UNOSAT Sentinel-1 SAR · Sentinel-2 optical Kyiv Oblast, Ukraine · 2022
Key result

A simple SAR intensity log-ratio, run on freely available Sentinel-1 imagery, correctly flagged 58% of UNOSAT's expert-labelled damaged buildings in Bucha (84 of 145) — rising to 76% once very small buildings are excluded — without needing any commercial high-resolution imagery or access to the ground.

The question

Why estimate war damage from orbit?

Conflict zones are, almost by definition, places researchers and humanitarian responders cannot safely enter. Yet damage assessment is exactly what's needed first — to direct relief, to document infrastructure loss, and eventually to scope reconstruction. Commercial very-high-resolution imagery (WorldView, GeoEye) can resolve individual buildings, but it is expensive, not systematically collected everywhere, and the organizations doing visual damage surveys from it (like the UN's UNOSAT program) can only cover a fraction of the affected area in the time that matters.

Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 are different: free, globally and systematically collected every few days, and — for Sentinel-1 — collected through cloud cover and at night. That combination is what makes medium-resolution satellite data a credible first-pass tool for war damage assessment, even if it can't replace a detailed building-by-building survey.

The role of SAR & optical remote sensing

Two independent signals, one damage map

The approach combines two change-detection methods on two different sensors. On Sentinel-1 (C-band SAR), a building's radar backscatter depends on its geometry — an intact structure produces a strong, stable signal from double-bounce reflections off walls, roof, and ground. When a building partially or fully collapses, that backscatter structure changes abruptly, and the log-ratio of pre- and post-event intensity picks up the change as either a sharp increase or decrease, depending on building height and which side of it collapsed.

On Sentinel-2 (optical), a Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) texture analysis looks for places where the spatial pattern of brightness — not just its average value — changes between pre- and post-event scenes, which tends to flag larger, structurally damaged buildings well but is less sensitive to small or dark-roofed ones.

Both change layers are masked to built-up areas (using OpenStreetMap building footprints and the World Settlement Footprint) to suppress false positives from flooding, seasonal vegetation change, and other non-building land-cover change — then thresholded against a small reference area to find the value that best separates damaged from intact buildings before applying it across the full study region.

What it shows

Damage concentrated where the front line was

Across Kyiv Oblast, the SAR-detected damage concentrates almost entirely in the northwestern suburbs — Irpin, Bucha, and Hostomel — consistent with the documented attempt to encircle and capture Kyiv early in the invasion. Detected buildings ranged from 85 m² to over 74,000 m², mostly multi-story apartment blocks, government buildings, supermarkets, and warehouses; small, vegetation-shrouded single-story houses were largely missed by both sensors, a limitation of working at medium resolution.

Limitations

Where medium-resolution damage mapping falls short

01
Small, low-rise buildings are hard to see. At 10 m pixel spacing, a single-story house surrounded by trees rarely produces a clean enough signal change to be reliably flagged — most of the missed UNOSAT labels were exactly this kind of building.
02
Backscatter and texture changes aren't damage-specific. Flooding, seasonal vegetation change, and soil moisture shifts can mimic a damage signal; building masks help, but an incomplete or outdated footprint layer (OpenStreetMap, in an active war zone, is rarely current) lets some of these false positives through.
03
Roof material biases the optical channel. Dark roofs show little brightness change even when destroyed, since the GLCM texture method depends on a visible contrast shift — it systematically misses damage that the SAR channel still detects.
04
No reliable ground truth in an active conflict. Validation depends on a third party's visual survey of commercial imagery (UNOSAT), itself preliminary and unverified in the field — so the 58%/76% figures describe agreement with another remote estimate, not a confirmed accuracy.
Findings

What the comparison shows

01
SAR intensity outperforms optical texture for small-to-large damage, while optical texture is more conservative — fewer false positives, but only catches the largest, clearest cases.
02
Damage detection accuracy scales with building size: severe damage to larger buildings is detected far more reliably (64%) than possible/minor damage to small ones (47–50%).
03
Free, systematic Sentinel data can produce a usable first-pass damage map within days, well before any commercial tasking or field survey is possible — valuable as initial reference for humanitarian response, even with its known gaps.

Evaluating elsewhere

A newer method I'm now testing on other sites

The log-ratio/GLCM approach above isn't the only way to do this. A 2025 paper by Ballinger introduces the Pixel-Wise T-Test (PWTT) — a per-pixel statistical change-detection method on Sentinel-1 backscatter time series that needs no training data, yet rivals deep-learning damage classifiers (AUC up to 0.88) while staying lightweight enough to run over an entire country in Google Earth Engine. It also leans on machine learning at the data layer: the building footprints it validates against come from Microsoft's deep-learning-derived global building dataset, and its accuracy is benchmarked directly against a CNN + random-forest pipeline. I'm currently testing this approach on a few of my other test sites to see how it compares with the SAR log-ratio method used above.
