Selected work · 03

Corner-reflector + GNSS validation

Benchmarking ascending and descending InSAR against GPS — and finding out why one track wins.

Validation Asc vs. Desc GNSS @ CR sites
Key result

Descending velocities track GNSS closely; ascending doesn't. The gap isn't random — it's coherence. Below about 0.6, the ascending track's agreement with GNSS falls apart.

The question

Do the velocities hold up — and which track do you trust?

InSAR can produce a beautiful velocity field that's quietly wrong. The only way to know is to check it against independent ground truth. And when ascending and descending tracks disagree over the same ground, you need a principled reason to believe one over the other.

This study puts both tracks on trial against GNSS at corner-reflector sites, then asks what separates the good agreement from the bad.

Data & approach

Radar against ground truth

At corner-reflector sites co-located with continuous GNSS, InSAR line-of-sight velocities are compared against the GNSS velocity projected into each track's line of sight — separately for ascending and descending. Agreement is then stratified by coherence to find what drives the differences.

What it shows

One track hugs the 1:1 line

Plotting InSAR against GNSS at each site makes the difference obvious: descending points sit tight on the one-to-one line, while ascending scatters around it. The scatter isn't noise to shrug at — it maps onto where coherence is low.

InSAR vs. GNSS line-of-sight velocity at corner-reflector sites (illustrative). Descending hugs the 1:1 line; ascending scatters — a spread that low coherence largely accounts for.
Findings

What the comparison reveals

01
Descending agrees with GNSS within tight scatter; ascending is markedly noisier over the same sites.
02
Coherence explains the gap — a threshold around 0.6 separates trustworthy agreement from poor agreement on the ascending track.
03
Where coherence holds, the tracks converge, which is what makes a vertical decomposition defensible.
