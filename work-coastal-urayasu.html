Selected work · 03

Coastal subsidence in Urayasu, Japan

Twenty-four years of multi-sensor InSAR over a reclaimed coastal city in Tokyo Bay, and what three different SAR wavelengths agree and disagree on.

● 1993 – 2017 ERS-1/-2 · ALOS PALSAR · ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 Validated against leveling
Key result

Three independent SAR missions spanning 1993–2017 all resolve the same reclaimed-land subsidence pattern in Urayasu City, peaking at ≈27 mm/yr (1993–2006), ≈30 mm/yr (2006–2010) and ≈18 mm/yr (2014–2017), and all agree with independent leveling surveys.

The question

Why monitor subsidence in coastal cities?

Coastal cities built on young, water-saturated, or artificially reclaimed ground are some of the most subsidence-prone places on Earth — New Orleans, Jakarta, Bangkok, Ho Chi Minh City, Shanghai, Venice, and the western Netherlands all share the same underlying vulnerability: soft, compressible deposits that slowly consolidate under their own weight and the load of the city built on top of them. Where that subsidence intersects sea level, the risk compounds — increased flood exposure, accelerated infrastructure fatigue, and in earthquake-prone settings, an added layer of liquefaction and post-seismic settlement risk.

That compounding risk is not a distant scenario. Between 1993 and 2023, global mean sea level rose by over 100 mm — and the rate is accelerating. In the western Pacific, local sea level rose by as much as 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) over the same period. When the ground beneath a coastal city sinks at 18–30 mm/yr while the ocean simultaneously rises, the relative sea level experienced at street level can climb at two to three times the global-average rate — dramatically increasing flood exposure ahead of any global projection.

Urayasu City, in Chiba Prefecture on Tokyo Bay, is a textbook case: more than 70% of its area was reclaimed from the bay between 1964 and 1980 using dredged sand and soil, it sits in an active seismic zone, and it was significantly affected by liquefaction-induced settlement during the 2011 Tohoku earthquake. Continuous, citywide deformation monitoring there is not an academic exercise — it is the basis for understanding which districts are still settling, why, and how fast.

The role of InSAR

One signal, three sensors, twenty-four years

Traditional leveling and GPS surveys are accurate but sparse, covering only a few dozen benchmark points across an entire city. Multi-temporal InSAR (persistent scatterer interferometry and the small baseline subset technique) turns repeat SAR acquisitions into dense, spatially continuous velocity fields, recoverable retroactively from satellite archives that predate any dedicated monitoring program.

This study chained together three SAR missions with different wavelengths to reconstruct a 24-year deformation record: C-band ERS-1/-2 (1993–2006), L-band ALOS PALSAR (2006–2010), and L-band ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 (2014–2017). Each stack was processed independently and tied to GPS and a shared 5 m DEM, then validated directly against an independent leveling survey network maintained by Chiba Prefecture since 1993.

Some of the results

The same districts, three decades apart

The peak subsidence rate shifted across the three observation windows, but the affected districts stayed largely the same: the border between Naka-Machi and Shin-Machi, and the coastal levee districts of Maihama, Hinode, and Akemi. These are all areas with the thickest layers of soft alluvial clay beneath the reclaimed fill. The naturally formed Moto-Machi district, by contrast, stayed essentially stable across all three periods.

Limitations

Where InSAR struggles in coastal settings

01
Decorrelation over vegetation and water. Urayasu's parks, street trees, and waterfront reduce coherence, especially for shorter-wavelength C-band (ERS-1/-2) data. Measurement-point density was visibly sparser there than over L-band PALSAR, which tolerates vegetated terrain better.
02
Cross-sensor comparison is not apples-to-apples. Different wavelengths, incidence angles, revisit intervals, and image counts across ERS-1/-2, PALSAR, and PALSAR-2 mean a sensor "disagreement" can reflect instrument characteristics as much as a real change in the ground.
03
Coarse temporal sampling misses short-term, human-driven motion. Long revisit times and a linear inversion model smooth out short bursts of settlement from construction or redevelopment — clearly the case in the continuously redeveloped Maihama/Tokyo Disney Resort district.
04
Archive gaps break continuity. The multi-year data gap between PALSAR and PALSAR-2, and missing ERS-1 acquisitions in 1994–1995, leave the long-term record stitched together from incomplete pieces rather than one continuous time series.
05
LOS-to-vertical conversion assumes negligible horizontal motion. Converting line-of-sight velocity to vertical subsidence by the incidence-angle cosine is a simplification that can bias results where lateral ground movement is non-trivial.
Findings

What 24 years of radar shows

01
Subsidence concentrates over thick alluvial clay beneath reclaimed fill, correlating spatially with a geologic map of stratum depth — natural soil consolidation, not just earthquake liquefaction, is the dominant long-term driver.
02
The subsidence rate has been decreasing over time citywide, from ~30 mm/yr in the PALSAR era down to ~18 mm/yr by 2014–2017, consistent with progressive consolidation settling toward equilibrium.
03
Independent leveling data confirms the InSAR trend at the millimetre level, with RMSE as low as 0.9 mm/yr. This gives confidence that satellite-only monitoring can substitute for dense ground survey networks in similar coastal cities.
