Selected work · 01

Basin-wide subsidence, Australia

Mapping vertical land motion across coal-seam-gas provinces at selected locations with a decade of Sentinel-1 radar.

● Ongoing Sentinel-1 IW 2017 – 2025 Eastern Australia
Key result

Sentinel-1 resolves a coherent, spatially continuous subsidence signal over producing fields at a millimetre-per-year scale. While traditional ground networks only see isolated points, satellite radar reveals the complete picture. At key sites, peak rates exceed −62 mm/yr within distinct deformation bowls.

The question

Is the ground moving, and where?

Australia's coal-seam-gas provinces host some of the largest resource extraction operations in the Southern Hemisphere. Producing gas means drawing down groundwater pressure across broad areas, which can translate into slow, spatially coherent vertical land motion at the surface — exactly the kind of signal that is hard to characterise with sparse ground instruments but well-suited to satellite radar.

The goal here is a basin-wide, spatially continuous picture of how much the ground is moving at selected locations, and how that deformation pattern relates to production activity — at a resolution and spatial coverage that no point network can provide. Results from individual sites are presented as example windows into what the full analysis reveals.

Data & approach

A decade of radar, calibrated and validated

Ascending and descending Sentinel-1 stacks are processed into line-of-sight velocity time series using SBAS, then tied to the ground with corner reflectors for calibration and cross-checked against continuous GNSS. Long-wavelength trends are constrained so that basin-scale signal is not lost to orbital or atmospheric ramps. At each selected site, the velocity field is analysed alongside the DEM to separate topographic influence from subsidence driven by subsurface processes.

What it shows

Deformation bowls in the landscape

At this example site, two spatially distinct subsidence bowls are visible in the InSAR velocity field, overlaid on the local DEM. The bowls are coherent and well-defined, with subsidence contours reaching −5, −10, and −20 mm/yr around a peak of −51.8 mm/yr. The topographic setting shows that the most rapidly subsiding pixels cluster at lower to mid elevations — consistent with a process tied to subsurface fluid extraction rather than surface processes.

(a) Digital Elevation Model of the study area (360–430 m asl), with topographic contours. The star marks the minimum velocity location. (b) InSAR LOS velocity map with subsidence contours at −5, −10, and −20 mm/yr, derived from Sentinel-1 ascending acquisitions. Two distinct deformation bowls (yellow boxes) are clearly resolved. n = 20,693 pixels; peak: −51.8 mm/yr; mean: −1.8 mm/yr; 61% of pixels are subsiding.
(c) LOS velocity vs. elevation. A weak positive trend (0.09 mm/yr per m, R = 0.24) indicates lower terrain subsides more rapidly, pointing to a subsurface source rather than a topographic artefact. 659 pixels exceed −10 mm/yr ("deep subsidence"). (d) E–W and N–S profiles through the subsidence centre, co-plotted with the DEM profile (dashed). The bowl reaches −52 mm/yr at its deepest point; the asymmetric flanks reflect the spatial geometry of the underlying structure.
3-D rotation
Three-dimensional view of the LOS velocity field draped over the DEM and rotated to show the spatial extent and depth of the subsidence bowl. The animation makes it clear that the deformation is not a DEM artefact — the bowl sits within a topographic low but extends well beyond the terrain feature.
Findings

What the radar reveals

01
Coherent, spatially structured subsidence concentrates in two distinct bowls rather than scattering randomly — a clear spatial fingerprint of a subsurface process, not surface noise.
02
Peak rates exceed −50 mm/yr at this example site, with 659 pixels subsiding faster than −10 mm/yr. The full basin-wide picture across all selected locations is still being compiled.
03
Velocity correlates weakly with elevation (R = 0.24, 0.09 mm/yr per m): lower-lying terrain subsides more, consistent with fluid withdrawal from below rather than a topographic or atmospheric artefact.
04
Ascending and descending geometries agree where coherence holds, providing confidence in the velocity decomposition and ruling out look-angle-specific artefacts.
